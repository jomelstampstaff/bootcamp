1.1 What Is a Mortgage? Session 1

A mortgage is a written contract between the mortgagor (the borrower) and the mortgagee (the lender) that requires the borrower to pledge their home as collateral for repayment of the loan.

Simple version: A mortgage is a legal IOU — the borrower receives a loan in exchange for a title lien and promises to pay it back on set terms (e.g., 30-year or 15-year at a fixed rate), with payments typically due on the 1st of each month.

If the borrower fails to meet the payment terms, the ultimate consequence is foreclosure — the security property is taken and sold by the lender. That's why the home is considered security or collateral.

1.2 Loan Types & Liens Session 1

First Mortgage

The first lien to be paid off from the sale or foreclosure of the secured property. Simple and senior.

Subordinate Liens

When there is more than one lien against the property (most commonly multiple mortgages). Subordinate liens are paid after the first mortgage is satisfied. A subordinate mortgage can be a closed-end second or a HELOC.

  • Closed-End Second Mortgage — a subordinate lien with an initial balance paid over a loan term. Like a first mortgage, just with another loan ahead of it in line. Fixed or variable rate; set monthly due date.
The Burger Line Analogy: Two people order the same burger. The kitchen makes the first one and realizes there's no second. The first person (first mortgage) gets paid. The second person (second mortgage) placed the order, paid for it, and walks away with nothing — a loss to write off.

1.3 Sale of a Home & Short Sale Session 1

Two common types of sales in today's market:

  • Traditional Sale — selling the home for at least what you owe (ideally more than what you paid).
  • Short Sale — the house is worth less than what is owed. The seller asks the bank to accept less than the loan balance. Requires a written agreement from the bank to accept $X in repayment.
Scenario A — Shortfall
Sale price: $200,000
1st mortgage owed: $185,000
2nd mortgage owed: $50,000
$200,000 − $185,000 = $15,000 remaining
2nd lender receives $15,000 → loss of $35,000
Scenario B — Both Paid in Full
Sale price: $200,000
1st mortgage owed: $150,000
2nd mortgage owed: $20,000
Both liens paid in full; $30,000 remains to seller
Seller profit = $30,000
On a mortgage note, if a foreclosure sale doesn't produce enough to cover the second mortgage, the second-mortgage lender is stuck with whatever they are given.

1.4 HELOCs Session 1

Home Equity Line of Credit (HELOC) — a revolving line of credit secured by the home. Works like a credit card:

  • Borrower gets a set maximum limit.
  • Borrower draws only what they need.
  • Repayments are made only on the amount drawn — not on the full available line.
Example: $100,000 HELOC limit, borrower draws $10,000 → payments are on the $10,000, not the $100,000.

1.5 Borrower, Co-Borrower, Buyer, Seller Session 1

Borrower & Co-Borrower

Applies to both purchases and refinances. These are the individuals who become obligated on the mortgage note once the closing documents are executed.

  • Each borrower completes their own 1003 / URLA.
  • Married couples mark joint credit on both applications.
  • Two friends, parent/child, or any other relationship → each on their own 1003, each considered a borrower.
  • If a married couple + a single friend are on the loan → the couple marks joint credit; the single person marks individual credit.
The borrower who earns the most is often (but not always) the primary borrower. Always look at the full picture.

Buyer & Seller

  • Buyer — the party purchasing the property per the sales contract.
  • Seller — selling the property to the borrower; must be the vested owner(s) on title.
Vested owner means they legally own the property. If someone else tries to sell on the owner's behalf and their name is on the contract instead of the actual title holder, the contract is invalid.

1.6 Credit Risk & FICO Session 1

Credit risk is the inherent risk to the lender based on the borrower's previous credit performance, DTI, LTV, marketability of collateral, and other factors.

FICO Scores

  • A score verified via a tri-merge credit report (Experian, Equifax, TransUnion).
  • Range: 300–850. Higher is better.
  • Factors: payment timeliness, balances vs. limits, DTI when applying for credit.
Mid-FICO Rule 3 scores → take the MIDDLE one
Two-Borrower Rule Use the LOWER of the two mid-FICO scores
Borrower A scores: 750 / 780 / 600 → mid = 600
Co-Borrower B scores: 720 / 700 / 690 → mid = 700
Lower of (600, 700) = 600
Qualifying FICO = 600

1.7 Reading a Credit Report Session 1

Our jobs are due diligence. Verify everything — the credit report is where most human errors hide (typos on SSN, misspelled last names).

Real example: A co-worker's file went all the way to closing before someone caught a wrong SSN on the credit report. The report ran under the wrong person, and the resulting FICO (though still qualifying) delayed the closing.

Key Sections to Review

  • Header / Scores — FICO, factors ("credit key factors"), reasons for low scores.
  • Mortgage History — 1st and 2nd mortgages, HELOCs. Look for any 30/60/90-day lates.
  • Auto Loans vs. Leases — an auto loan with 10 payments or less may be excluded from DTI. A lease cannot be excluded.
  • Derogatory Section — all lates, how many days, when, and what type of loan. Check against program guidelines.
  • Public Records — bankruptcies, judgments (note: since 2017 most judgments and tax liens are no longer reported — require extra scrutiny elsewhere).
  • Inquiries — too many recent inquiries can indicate financial distress.

1.8 LTV, CLTV & HCLTV Session 1

Refinance: always use current appraised value (check guidelines if purchased in last 12 months).
Purchase: always use the lower of purchase price or final appraised value.
Loan-to-Value (LTV) 1st Mortgage Loan Amount ÷ Lower(Appraised Value, Purchase Price)
Combined LTV (CLTV) (1st Loan + Subordinate Balance) ÷ Lower(Appraised Value, Purchase Price)
High Combined LTV (HCLTV) (1st Loan + Subordinate MAX LIMIT) ÷ Lower(Appraised Value, Purchase Price)
Example 1
Appraised value: $250,000 | Purchase price: $300,000
Use $250,000 (lower)
1st loan: $150,000 → LTV = 150,000 ÷ 250,000 = 60%
HELOC balance: $50,000 (limit $60,000)
CLTV = (150,000 + 50,000) ÷ 250,000 = 80%
HCLTV = (150,000 + 60,000) ÷ 250,000 = 84%
Example 2
Purchase price: $250,000 | Appraised value: $275,000
1st loan: $175,000 | HELOC balance: $50,000 | HELOC limit: $80,000
LTV = 175,000 ÷ 250,000 = 70%
CLTV = (175,000 + 50,000) ÷ 250,000 = 90%
HCLTV = (175,000 + 80,000) ÷ 250,000 = 102%
A 102% HCLTV is a red flag — most guidelines don't allow it. It indicates outstanding liens exceed the home's worth.

1.9 PITIA & Debt-to-Income Session 1

PITIA = the monthly housing payment

  • P — Principal
  • I — Interest
  • T — Taxes (property)
  • I — Insurance (homeowners, flood if required, mortgage insurance if required)
  • A — Association dues (HOA — common on condos, co-ops, PUDs)
Housing Ratio (Front-End DTI) PITIA ÷ Total Monthly Qualifying Income
Total DTI (Back-End) (PITIA + All Other Debts) ÷ Total Monthly Qualifying Income
PITIA components: $650 + $250 + $300 + $75 + $67.50
Total PITIA = $1,342.50
Other debts: $300 (child support) + $50 (revolving) + $400 (auto) + $150 (student) = $900
Total debt = $1,342.50 + $900 = $2,242.50
Qualifying income = $4,500
Housing ratio = 1,342.50 ÷ 4,500 = 29.83%
Total DTI = 2,242.50 ÷ 4,500 = 49.83%
The total DTI (back-end ratio) is the most important ratio used for guideline qualification.

1.10 Purchase & Refinance Terms Session 1

Purchase Agreement

Written contract between seller and buyer outlining the terms. Contains:

  • Property address + all party names (buyer, seller, agents, attorneys)
  • Sales price + earnest money deposit (check, money order, or wire)
  • Seller contributions toward buyer's closing costs
  • Three key dates: contract execution, mortgage commitment due date, expected closing date
  • Any repairs required before closing (health/safety repairs need proof before final approval)

Down Payment & Closing Costs

  • Down Payment = Purchase Price − Loan Amount
  • Closing Costs — credit report, appraisal, title, origination fees
  • Prepaids — initial escrow deposits, current taxes/insurance due
  • Escrow Account — lender-maintained, borrower-funded; think of it as a savings account under the borrower's name used to pay taxes and insurance.

Refinance Types

  • Rate & Term (Limited Cash-Out / LCO) — pays off existing mortgage(s); purchase-money subordinate liens may also be paid off.
  • Cash-Out Refinance — takes equity out of the home; pays off 1st mortgage, can pay off non-purchase-money subordinate liens, consumer debts, or give cash in hand. Can even be done on a free-and-clear home.
When refinancing, order a payoff statement — tells you exactly what's owed and the "good through" date after which the payoff changes.

1.11 Cash to Close Session 1

Cash to Close (Down Payment + Closing Costs + Prepaids) − Credits

Credits include:

  • Earnest Money Deposit (EMD) — buyer funds given at contract acceptance; must be sourced from borrower's own accounts. Cash is not acceptable.
  • Lender Credit — a paper-trail credit on the closing disclosure; reduces cash needed.
  • Interested Party Contribution (IPC) — contribution from seller, agent, or attorney.
  • Seller Concessions — excessive IPCs or non-realty contributions (furniture, moving costs, decor allowances).
Each agency (Fannie, Freddie, FHA, VA, USDA) sets specific dollar or percentage limits on IPCs and concessions. Always verify against the program.
Purchase Example
Purchase price: $250,000 | Loan: $200,000
Down payment = 250,000 − 200,000 = $50,000
Closing costs + prepaids = $7,500
Cash to close before credits = 50,000 + 7,500 = $57,500
Credits: EMD $1,000 + IPC $3,000 = $4,000
Cash to close after credits = 57,500 − 4,000 = $53,500
Refinance Example
New loan: $300,000 | 1st payoff: $250,000 | 2nd payoff (non-purchase-money): $45,000 | Costs: $6,000
Total payoffs + costs = 250,000 + 45,000 + 6,000 = $301,000
$301,000 > $300,000 → no cash back
Cash to close = $1,000 (from verified borrower funds)
Because the 2nd mortgage was not purchase money, this scenario is a cash-out refinance, not rate-and-term.

1.12 Loan Programs Session 1

Conventional

  • Underwritten to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac guidelines.
  • Conforming = loan amount meets Fannie/Freddie county limits.
  • PMI (Private Mortgage Insurance) required when LTV > 80%. Protects the lender if the borrower defaults — not the borrower.

USDA (Rural Housing Development)

  • Government mortgage insurance program for lower-income borrowers.
  • Property must be in a designated rural area.
  • Borrowers must meet maximum household income limits.

FHA (Federal Housing Administration)

  • Government mortgage insurance program.
  • Lower down payments than most conventional.
  • Less stringent on credit guidelines.
  • Always has monthly mortgage insurance.

VA (Veterans Administration)

  • Government mortgage guarantee program for veterans of the U.S. Armed Forces.
  • Allows purchase with no down payment.
All government-backed programs allow little-to-no down payment in exchange for giving lenders a higher insurance guarantee on riskier loans.

Session 1 Practice Quiz 10 Questions

1. What does PITIA stand for?

Principal, Interest, Taxes, Homeowners Insurance, Mortgage Insurance (if required), and Homeowners Association Dues (if required).

2. What are the three credit bureaus that make up a tri-merge credit report?

Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion.

3. True or false — a seller concession can be any dollar amount.

False. FHA, Conventional, VA, and USDA all have specific limits (often percentage-based on loan amount and property type).

4. What are the funds called that are given to the seller's realtor or attorney at the time the sales contract is signed?

Earnest Money Deposit (EMD). Must be sourced from an acceptable source (personal/cashier's check or wire — never cash).

5. What type of refinance is it when the borrower only pays off the current mortgage?

Rate and Term refinance.

6. What loan program is geared towards veterans of the United States?

VA (Veterans Administration).

7. True or false — the loan closer issues the clear to close and then drafts the closing disclosures for the borrower to sign at closing.

False (trick question). Only the underwriter has authority to issue clear-to-close. The loan closer drafts the disclosures.

8. A ______ originates a loan, pledges the loan to a lender, and closes it in the lender's name.

Broker.

9. What is the PITIA amount from the chart in this session?

$1,342.50 = $650 + $250 + $300 + $75 + $67.50.

10. If the borrower is buying a home at a purchase price of $400,000 and putting down 20%, how much is the down payment in dollars?

$80,000 ($400,000 × 20%).

2.1 Underwriter Duties Session 2

Every loan file you touch requires you to:

  • Evaluate the Four C's of underwriting — Credit, Capacity, Capital, Collateral — and the credit risk of each loan file.
  • Determine whether the loan file meets product and program guidelines.
  • Identify red flags or fraudulent activity.
  • Determine whether the loan poses an acceptable risk.
  • Issue the loan decision: conditional approval, suspense, denial, and/or clear-to-close.
  • Meet production expectations for new submissions and cleared conditions.
Reality check: Most lenders don't publish a rigid "X loans per day" quota because every file is different. A 10-business-return self-employed file with a multi-property co-borrower could eat a full day; three simple W-2 files might be the same effort. Set expectations with your manager — advocate for yourself if roadblocks persist.

2.2 Underwriter Responsibilities Session 2

  • Meet industry standards for loan quality — the accuracy of verified data in the underwriting decision compared to the documentation provided.
  • Meet rush request deadlines as approved by management (common on purchases with contract deadlines).
  • Never let a rush diminish the quality of your review. All parties — UW, processor, LO — must prioritize due diligence.
  • Provide timely customer service to internal customers: loan officers, processors, managers, closers, QC associates, post-closing, and secondary marketing.
Time management tip: If you can't pick up your phone mid-file, let it go to voicemail and return calls at your next stopping point. Better yet — send a quick email: "Just saw your call — working on something, will call back." Always leave a paper trail.
If LOs get nervous when you don't answer, they'll escalate to their manager, who calls your manager. That turns into a big issue fast. Responsiveness is part of the job.

2.3 Underwriter Skill Sets Session 2

What you must be able to do:

  • Evaluate AUS findings, reports, and research product/program guidelines.
  • Identify adverse credit events and determine acceptable seasoning and credit rebuilding.
  • Calculate wage earner, fixed income, rental, and self-employment income, plus other income sources.
  • Evaluate depository, investment, and retirement assets for cash to close and reserves.
  • Review appraisals and additional valuation forms — market value and subject property condition.
  • Verify data integrity through the LOS (loan origination system) and AUS.
When you get challenged — "the DU only asks for one bank statement, why are you conditioning two?" — it may be a simple answer. Read the guideline carefully; explain your reasoning clearly.
Golden rule: If you don't know, ask. Co-workers, supervisors, internal help desks — all fair game. Don't guess on a credit decision.

2.4 Underwriting Authority & Certifications Session 2

Underwriting authority is the credit decision authorization granted to the underwriter by their employer. There are different levels:

Training Program Certificates

Every job starts with training and exams to certify you can underwrite for that company.

FHA Direct Endorsement (DE)

  • Requires a CHUMS ID number.
  • Without it, you cannot underwrite FHA loans — you're limited to conventional.

VA Credit Underwriting Authority (SAR)

  • Required to underwrite VA loans.
  • Without it, VA loans must be underwritten by someone with the certification.

Conventional Delegated Authority

  • Covers standard, jumbo, and Non-QM loans (varies by lender).
Critical rule: If you do not feel the loan is supported, do not use your authority signature to approve it. Seek a second opinion or a senior underwriter's signature. On FHA/VA, your name is on the file — if the loan defaults and the decision comes into question, it points back to you.
Loan notes in your review should explain how you determined the borrowers can repay. If the data is correct and the loan meets guidelines, your decision can be justified.

2.5 Underwriting Tools Session 2

What you use daily:

  • Underwriting guidelines — agency, company, and investor.
  • Company / agency / investor memos and policy updates.
  • Underwriting worksheets — wage earner, rental, self-employment tax returns.
  • Loan file notations and write-ups.

Income Worksheets

Every company has a different income worksheet. Some use Fannie/Freddie standard forms, some build their own. Key inputs:

  • Underwriter name, review date, loan number, borrower and employer names.
  • Income type: hourly, salary, social security, pension, child support, etc.
  • One or two borrower sections — use as many forms as needed.
  • Wage calculation: hourly × hours, year-to-date check against base earnings.
  • Handles salary, bonus, commission, overtime.
If your employer doesn't provide worksheets, grab them from the Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac selling guides online.

2.6 Agency Guidelines & Overlays Session 2

Guidelines are the tools that help us underwrite a file. Every lender and every program has its own set.

Where to find them

  • Fannie Mae Selling Guide — fanniemae.com
  • Freddie Mac Selling Guide — freddiemac.com
  • FHA Handbook — HUD.gov
  • VA Lender's Handbook — benefits.va.gov
  • USDA Handbook — rd.usda.gov

Overlays

Additional lender or investor requirements stacked on top of agency guidelines. Example: Fannie says 1 month of bank statements, but your investor requires 2. That's an overlay.

Always check both the agency guideline and your investor's overlay. Never assume agency minimums are the floor.
Many lenders use a paid site (like AllRegs / ORGS) that consolidates agency + investor guidelines in one place. Ask your employer if you have access — then practice navigating it.

2.7 Automated vs. Manual Underwriting Session 2

Automated (AUS)

Takes the data you input — credit, income, assets, SSN, address, purchase price, loan amount, debts — and returns a decision: Approve, Refer, Ineligible, or Manual Review required.

Manual Underwriting

Us. We physically review the file and make the decision, checking every program component against guidelines.

Manual underwriting is usually reserved for:
  • Borrowers with limited or no credit
  • Bankruptcies (especially Chapter 13)
  • Inaccurate data on the credit profile
Most lenders don't do manual conventional underwrites. Manual is more common for government loans. Always confirm with your employer when manual is permitted.

AUS by Agency

AgencyAUS System
Fannie MaeDU — Desktop Underwriter
Freddie MacLPA — Loan Product Advisor
USDAGUS — Guaranteed Underwriting System
FHATotal Scorecard (run through DU or LPA)
Some lenders use only DU for conventional, some only LPA, some use both, and some have proprietary systems. Confirm which systems you're expected to use.

2.8 AUS Findings Overview Session 2

The AUS evaluates several main credit risk factors:

  • Credit history — delinquencies, mortgage accounts, foreclosures.
  • Revolving credit utilization — how much of available credit is drawn. Maxed-out cards indicate financial stress.
  • Public records — collections, tax liens, judgments.
  • Inquiries — too many inquiries in a short window can lower your score (borrower looks "needy for money").

And these eligibility factors:

  • LTV, CLTV, HCLTV
  • Post-closing liquid reserves
  • Loan purpose — why is the borrower refinancing? Is there a tangible benefit?
  • Loan term and amortization (30-yr → 15-yr, or vice versa — an LOE may be required)
  • Total expense ratios (DTI)
  • Property type
  • Co-borrower — who are they to the primary? Where do they live?

2.9 DU Recommendations Session 2

Approve / Eligible

What you want. Loan satisfies Fannie Mae credit risk standards AND eligibility. Follow the conditions, verify overlays, and you should be good to close.

Refer / Eligible

Loan may be eligible but requires a manual underwrite. Manual may not be allowed on some conventional loans. If you can meet manual guidelines and confirm acceptable risk, you can overturn the AUS decision.

Ineligible

Does not satisfy Fannie Mae eligibility criteria. Often simply incorrect data entry, or an aspect of the submission (LTV too high, missing assets, etc.). Review the findings and correct.

Refer with Caution

Loan does not meet credit risk or eligibility standards and is ineligible for delivery. Rarely overturned or manually underwritten. Verify data entry first; if the result holds, most lenders cannot do the loan.

Out of Scope

Uncommon. DU is unable to underwrite the loan as submitted — clear indicator of wrong data entry or an ineligible program for DU.

"Garbage in, garbage out." If the data you input is inaccurate, the feedback messaging will be wrong and you won't get the right findings.

2.10 LPA Findings Session 2

LPA is very similar to DU but uses different verbiage:

Key LPA Definitions

  • Accept — satisfies Freddie Mac guidelines and credit risk standards.
  • Streamlined Accept — approval with reduced documentation (appraisal waiver, one bank statement, fewer pay stubs).
  • Standard — meets eligibility; good to go with listed docs.
  • Caution — unlikely to meet Freddie Mac eligibility due to layered risk and/or data errors.
  • Ineligible / Incomplete / Invalid — LPA can't underwrite as submitted. Review data entry.
LPA tends to be more finicky when transferring data from systems like Encompass. If fields aren't mapped correctly, you'll get inaccurate findings until they're corrected.

2.11 Data Integrity & AUS Limitations Session 2

Data integrity = accuracy of data input into the AUS. Inaccurate input = inaccurate findings. "Garbage in, garbage out."

AUS Limitations

  • It reads factual data only — it cannot interpret context.
  • It cannot read a pay stub. If a child support deduction appears on a pay stub but isn't manually added as a liability, the DTI will be wrong.
  • It may flag a late but won't know the reason — even when a guideline allows an exception for a documented reason.
  • Different AI engines give different answers based on the same flawed input.
Classic example: Pay stub shows a child support garnishment. The AUS will not add it unless you input it into the loan system as a liability. If you don't, the AUS findings will show an incorrect DTI — and the loan could close without the debt ever being counted.

2.12 Form 1003 / URLA Session 2

The Uniform Residential Loan Application (URLA) is the loan application. This is your go-to source for all loan data.

URLA Sections

  • Section 1 — Borrower Information (1a–1e): Personal info and expanded income sources. Now asks if the borrower is self-employed and has a separate section for income from other sources.
  • Section 2 — Financial Information: Assets & Liabilities (2a–2d): Bank accounts, investments, sale of real estate, credit cards, auto loans, alimony, child support. Note: Section 2d no longer requires the borrower to disclose alimony/child support on the declaration.
  • Section 3 — Financial Information: Real Estate (3a–3c): All real estate owned, mortgages, rental income, HELOCs, 2nd mortgages. 3a is for subject property (refi) or primary residence (when subject is a 2nd home or investment purchase).
  • Section 4 — Loan & Property Information (4a–4d): Subject property, additional financing, rental income from subject, gifts/grants.
  • Section 5 — Declarations (5a–5b): Split into subject-property/funds (5a) and borrower financial info (5b). Borrower no longer declares whether they're subject to withholding or liable for alimony/child support.
  • Section 6 — Acknowledgments & Agreements: Legal obligations, representations, authorizations.
  • Section 7 — Military Service: New section — provides data to GSEs on availability of financing for veterans.
  • Section 8 — Demographic Information: Replaces the old HMDA section. Voluntary.
  • Section 9 — Loan Originator Information: LO contact info, NMLS ID, state license number. Must be signed and dated by LO.
The new URLA became effective January 2021 and required as of March 1, 2021. It was designed to better interface with DU and LPA.
Anytime you change anything on your 1003 (income, assets, liabilities), re-run your AUS so you get the most accurate approval status.

2.13 Form 1008 Session 2

The Uniform Underwriting and Transmittal Summary is the underwriter's loan summary. Reflects your PITIA, DTI, LTV, credit approval status, AUS used, and/or manual decision.

Section 1 — Borrower & Property

Borrower info, property info, mortgage type.

Section 2 — Mortgage Information

Loan type, amortization type, loan purpose, lien position, note info, etc.

Section 3 — Underwriting Details

How you underwrote the loan. This is the most important section for the file's future reviewer (investor, QC).

  • AUS used (DU / LPA / Manual) and the rating provided.
  • Underwriting comments — the thought process behind income calculations, appraisal notes, credit explanations.
  • Signature — required for manual underwrites (Fannie/FHA/VA); AUS approvals already state so on the form.
Quick tip: UW = underwriter / underwriting.

Example comment: "Underwriter verified child support income with a copy of the divorce decree and a bank statement showing direct deposits. Self-employment income — see income worksheet."
Every loan is sold or QC-reviewed down the road. The next person to pick up the file needs to know why you underwrote it the way you did.

2.14 DU & LPA Findings Walkthrough Session 2

DU Findings — Key Sections

  • Recommendation — borrower name, DU case file ID, submission date, number of submissions.
  • Mortgage Terms & Product — what you input.
  • Risk Eligibility Findings — why a loan got Refer or Ineligible. High-level guideline info.
  • Credit Report Expiration — old reports expire; you may need a new one.
  • Credit Liabilities — 30-day accounts, excluded debts, and why they're excluded.
  • Employment Information — how many pay stubs, W2s, tax returns required.
  • Assets — how many months of statements to source.
  • Appraisal — required or waived.
  • Underwriting Analysis Report — a synopsis of the whole file; compare against your 1008 to make sure they match.
Excessive AUS runs: The more times you resubmit, the higher the chance findings change. Excessive runs trigger red flags in your findings. Only resubmit when necessary.

LPA Findings — Key Sections

  • Assessment Summary — borrower name, submissions, LP Key number (unique ID).
  • Purchase Eligibility — eligible/ineligible, risk class, collateral.
  • Loan Details — borrower names, SSN, lender loan #, property address.
  • LTV, DTI, Credit Score — highlights the middle FICO used.
  • Message Summary — count of feedback messages per section.
  • Feedback Messages — purchase restriction, data quality, affordable lending, employment/income, assets/reserves, credit/liabilities, property/appraisal, general, mortgage insurance.
DU and LPA are the bare minimum conditions. Your investor overlays may require additional documentation.

2.15 Credit Report — Section by Section Session 2

Application Information

  • Borrower name, DOB, SSN, address history, employment history.
  • Verify against the SS card and driver's license. If the address on the license doesn't match the 1003, question it.

Credit Summary

  • Trade line mix, payment history, individual trade line payment history.
  • Collections and charge-offs.

Public Records

  • Bankruptcies — chapter, filing date.
  • Judgments and tax liens (note: since 2017 most no longer report — extra scrutiny needed elsewhere).

Inquiries

  • Who pulled the credit. Too many auto inquiries without a new auto loan showing up = red flag. Ask the borrower directly.

Fraud Alerts

  • Fraud holds prevent a bureau from returning scores. You may need to contact the borrower directly to verify identity.
  • Borrowers add these after experiencing past fraud — always respect the process.

Mortgage History & Derogatories

  • Look for 30/60/90-day lates. Guidelines often allow fewer than a set number of lates within a lookback window.
  • Note if a mortgage has never been more than 90 days late — that's a strong positive.

Auto Loans vs. Leases

  • Auto loan with 10 payments or less → may exclude the payment from DTI.
  • Lease payments cannot be excluded.
  • Watch for mis-reported leases showing up as auto loans. Adding them back later can break the qualification.

2.16 Mid-FICO Exercise Session 2

Borrower: 684 / 720 / 660
Sort: 660, 684, 720 → mid-FICO = 684
Co-Borrower: 745 / 750 / 720
Sort: 720, 745, 750 → mid-FICO = 745
Qualifying = lower of (684, 745)
Qualifying FICO = 684
Two scores only? Use the lower of the two.
Three scores? Use the middle — not highest, not lowest.
One score only? Check guidelines — some programs allow, some don't.

Session 2 Practice Quiz 10 Questions

1. True or false — underwriters only provide updates on the loan to the borrower and the loan officer.

False. Underwriters provide updates to internal employees (LO, processor, managers), but typically never contact the borrower directly.

2. For an underwriter to work on an FHA loan, what must he or she have?

FHA endorsement — a DE (Direct Endorsement) — with a CHUMS ID number.

3. True or false — working on a Fannie Mae loan, the AUS will be LPA.

False. Fannie Mae → DU. Freddie Mac → LPA.

4. A ______ DU recommendation indicates the loan does not satisfy Fannie Mae's loan eligibility criteria.

Ineligible.

5. Form ______ summarizes the loan characteristics that determined the loan decision.

Form 1008 (Uniform Underwriting and Transmittal Summary). Form 1003 summarizes loan information, not the decision.

6. Which FICO score would you use to qualify the loan with two borrowers and three scores each?

Lower of the two mid-FICO scores. In the exercise: borrower mid = 684, co-borrower mid = 745 → use 684.

7. Which FICO score would you use if the borrower only has two on the credit report?

The lower of the two.

8. What section of the credit report provides address history?

The applicant information section.

9. Where on the credit report can you find bankruptcies?

Public Records. Note: since 2017, most judgments and tax liens no longer report — verify elsewhere.

10. Does the underwriter do their own income calculation worksheet if the loan officer or processor did one already?

Yes. You own the file. You make the credit decision. You must verify the numbers yourself — don't just accept someone else's worksheet. Common example: an LO adds overtime income with no 2-year history — if you accept it, your file is wrong from the start.
